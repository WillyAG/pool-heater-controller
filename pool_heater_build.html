Pool Heater Probe Override

The heater's own probe now sits on the pool wall and is read by an ESP32-S3-Zero. The ESP32 decides when to heat and hands the heater controller one of two fake probe readings to turn it on or off. This page records the circuit, layout, wiring, calibration, installation and firmware as built.

Rev E: as builtInstalled Nov 12Firmware v1.7.1Perfboard 18 × 24 holes

01How it switches the heater

The probe is an NTC thermistor: lower resistance means hotter. The firmware closes exactly one PhotoMOS relay at a time, which puts either the HOT or the COLD resistor across the heater controller's probe input. The heater's own setpoint is set to 70 °F, midway between the two fake readings, and left there.

Changeover rule for the firmware: turn the new relay on, wait about 50 ms, then turn the old one off. The controller never sees an open sensor during normal switching.

Module header spacing (confirmed)

The ESP32-S3-Zero's header rows are 6 holes apart (0.6 in), measured on your board: the left header goes in column 2 and the right header in column 8. The pin labels match the GP6…5V order shown here.

  • USB-C faces the bottom edge so the power cable plugs in from outside the board. The module sits on female headers, so you can pull it and program it by USB if an over-the-air update ever goes wrong.
  • Antenna end (top of the module) is kept clear of parts and wiring on both sides. Don't mount the board with that end against the Pi or a metal surface.
  • Relays go in sockets. The dot (pin 1) is top-left on both.
  • Terminal blocks face the right edge so wires come straight in from the box's cable gland.
  • R1 is two resistors in series, both standing up: R1a in (10,12)–(11,12), R1b in (13,12)–(14,12), joined by a bare link (11,12)→(12,12)→(13,12) on the bottom. The ends are where the drawing shows R1.
  • R6 and R8 are plain wire links, 2 holes apart. If a value ever needs trimming, a resistor fits there mounted standing up.
  • J1 and J2 sit on the last column, so their screw side hangs slightly past the board edge. That is intended: wires go straight in from the side.

07Build and test order

  1. Solder the header sockets, the DIP sockets, J1, J2, all the resistors (with wire links in R6 and R8) and C1. Leave the module and relays out.
  2. Fit the bare links and insulated wires from section 04. With a meter, check continuity on each net, and check there is no short between 3V3, GND, PROBE and GP1.
  3. Plug in the module only, power it by USB, and load the first sketch (with over-the-air updates) at your desk.
  4. Put a 4.7k resistor in J1 instead of the probe. The web page should read about 74 °F. Try other resistors to watch the reading move.
  5. Insert K1 and K2. Put a meter set to resistance across J2. Switching HOT, COLD and off from the web page should read your HOT value, your COLD value and open circuit.
  6. Wire it to the heater with the heater's power off.

Bypass: if you use pluggable terminal blocks, you can return the heater to stock in seconds. Unplug both, and join the probe wires straight to the heater wires with a lever connector.

08Calibration record

PointProbeTemperatureReference
Ice bath13,820 Ω32.0 °Fpacked crushed ice and water
Warm water2,890 Ω96.3 °Ffever thermometer
  • Resulting curve: B = 3696, 4,444 Ω at 77 °F. Built into the firmware as the default since v1.2, so a fresh or wiped chip reads correctly without recalibrating.
  • Installed offset: about +3 °F, set with Calibrate… → Match reading against the bulb pool thermometer, after the probe had been on the wall for a while. Redo it if a fresh chip goes in.
  • Lesson learned: the multimeter's temperature probe reads about 9–10 °F high (41.7 °F in an ice bath). Use an ice bath, the fever thermometer (94–106 °F only) or the bulb thermometer as references instead.
  • The heater controller expects the same type of probe. The earlier "heater reads 10 °F cold" idea came from the meter's error, not the heater.

09Installation (as built)

  • Probe: on the outside of the steel pool wall, behind the 6 in insulation, about mid-depth and away from the return jet. Silicone heat-sink compound, then foam tape, insulation pushed back over it, wire taped down for strain relief.
  • Wiring: CAT-5. One pair from J1 to the probe, a separate pair from J2 to the heater controller's probe input. Splices soldered and sealed.
  • Heater: setpoint fixed at 70 °F. Its display now shows 89 °F (ESP32 says off) or 50 °F (ESP32 says heat), not the pool temperature. It still won't run without water flow.
  • Box: shares the plastic box with the Raspberry Pi, powered by its own 5 V USB-C supply.
  • Wi-Fi: connected through the pool-side extender (MAC address confirmed) at about -60 dBm. The heat pump's electrical noise is why the house router alone wasn't reliable out there.
  • Fail-safe tested: with the sensor input open, the heater shows P1 after a few seconds and stops.

10Firmware v1.7.1

Web page at http://poolheater.local (or the IP address on its Wi-Fi line), from a phone, tablet, PC or the Pi's desktop icon.

SettingMeaning
Setpoint °FThe heater stops after every reading has been at or above this for the confirm time.
Neg Differential °FThe heater restarts after every reading has been at or below setpoint minus this for the confirm time.
Confirm time (min)How long readings must hold before switching. Any reading on the wrong side restarts the timer.
Min. off time (min)Compressor protection: the shortest time between stopping and restarting.
Heating time windowOptional schedule. Ignored until the clock is set from the internet after a restart.
  • Readings are taken 4 times a second and compared at 0.1 °F, as shown, with no smoothing.
  • Temperature history: every 5 minutes the board saves the average, low, high and percentage of time heating to its spare flash memory. That's about 14 months (417 days); when it's full, the oldest records are replaced. History survives restarts, power cuts and firmware updates.
  • History graph: the mouse wheel zooms (1 hour up to everything stored), dragging or the slider moves through time, and buttons jump to Day, Week, Month, 3 months, Year or All. Hover for details.
  • Chip speed: 80 MHz (since v1.6), the slowest speed that keeps Wi-Fi, for less power and heat.
  • Force on goes back to Auto after 2 hours and after any restart. Force off is remembered.
  • Safety: a probe fault or water above 104 °F holds the heater off. A watchdog restarts the chip within 10 s if the program freezes, and it starts with the heater off.
  • Calibration and Wi-Fi changes are behind buttons with "Are you sure?" steps. New Wi-Fi lines in the code always take effect after an upload, and the board falls back to them if saved details stop working.
  • Updates: from the PC over Wi-Fi with the Arduino IDE (Tools → Port → poolheater). Board: ESP32S3 Dev Module, USB CDC On Boot enabled, Partition Default 4MB with spiffs (holds the history), Erase All Flash disabled (enabling it wipes settings and history). Wi-Fi and update passwords live in secrets.h next to PoolHeater.ino (copied from secrets.example.h), so a new version only replaces PoolHeater.ino. Keep only one .ino file in the sketch folder: the IDE combines every .ino file in that folder, so old copies cause "redefinition" errors. Older versions are kept in the GitHub history instead.
  • Spare chip: keep the second ESP32-S3-Zero loaded with the same version. To swap: plug it in, enter the settings and redo Match reading.
